Settings should make every choice feel natural.

The current interface exposes too much machinery and too often uses the wrong interaction for the choice. The next design needs clearer organization and a better way to make every decision.

Important changes to the earlier direction

Infer the actual screen size; remove Preview screen and Expand canvas. Replace Starting layout with visual templates. Remove Arrange neatly as a permanent action: it currently resets modules to equal-size tiles. Keep recovery available contextually instead of permanent Undo/Redo furniture. Show slideshow styles visually, separate accessory automation from accessory choice, and separate photo viewing from selection and editing.

The diagnosis

Capability accumulated faster than design.

The warm lightbox, readable type, generous targets, palette tiles and Momo artwork are good foundations. The discomfort comes from the decisions and relationships inside that shell. Setup, everyday preferences, content editing and maintenance often share the same visual weight.

1. Too many kinds of things are peers

Appearance, a layout editor, an event-review queue and software maintenance all occupy the same navigation level. “Photos” contains a miniature suite of tools. The rail reflects feature additions more than the owner’s mental model.

2. Forms are the default interaction

Choosing a hat duplicates a gallery with a dropdown. Cropping asks for two axes. Seasonal dates ask for numbers 1–12. These controls are individually implementable, but collectively make ownership feel technical.

3. Space does not establish hierarchy

Large fields and generous gaps give simple choices a whole screen, while critical or frequent tasks sit several scrolls below. Collapsed photo sections resemble headings, so even discovering what is interactive takes effort.

4. The frame asks us to reconcile rules

Retention and capacity overlap. Multiple night-time behaviors live in separate places. “Saved on this frame” sits above forms that still need Connect or Save. The owner must infer scope, dependencies and persistence.

The design objective: fewer decisions, clearer ownership, and better interactions. Moving every awkward control behind an accordion would preserve the underlying problem.

The “what would Apple do?” lens is useful as a principle, not a visual template. Apple’s Settings guidance favors fewer settings and sensible automatic behavior. For Skylit, my interpretation is to remove unnecessary decisions and place remaining controls where their effects are understandable. Apple Human Interface Guidelines: Settings.

Give each choice one clear home.

Start with seven recognizable destinations. The exact count is negotiable; the boundaries matter more. Within a destination, use short summaries and focused subpages. Let Settings open larger tools for larger tasks.

A preference can have more than one entry point, but only one editor and one saved value. Photos may link to automatic display in Frame; it should not contain a second copy of its controls.

Notes, shopping lists, countdowns and video shortcuts are content. Edit them in the relevant activity or in a task-focused lightbox opened from a widget. Settings may provide a shortcut to that tool. This preserves access without turning preferences into a content-management form.

“Move” must not mean “make hard to find”

Photo curation should remain accessible from Settings, as requested. “Manage photos” opens a spacious library tool, with a clear return to Photos settings. Slideshow configuration stays off the player. Android and wireless recovery remain reachable from Frame.

Responsive composition

Keep it usable at every landscape size.

The problem is not a need to make everything smaller. Photos still overwhelms a taller desktop. A shorter display makes misplaced hierarchy more visible. Preserve the frame’s readable scale while changing the structure.

Recommended sequence

Redesign the decisions before polishing the controls.

1

Define ownership and remove mechanics

Agree the destinations and the optimal interaction for each choice. Remove dashboard coordinates, the Preview screen and Expand workarounds, crop-axis sliders, numeric month entry and raw JSON from the normal owner UI. Eliminate duplicate Momo selectors and repeated page-visibility controls. Do this before restyling forms.

2

Build the two substantial workspaces

Give dashboard arrangement and photo-library management room to function. Provide direct manipulation, meaningful previews, contextual actions and recovery. Settings remains the launch point for both.

3

Make the everyday pages short and legible

Rebuild Appearance, Slideshow, Receiving and Location & time around clear summaries and appropriate choice controls. Give automatic behavior polished defaults. Expose customization only for a real owner goal.

4

Explain state and routine

Make configured, disabled, pending, failed and unavailable states distinct. Put daily behaviors together with an intelligible timeline/summary. Preserve existing schedules, retention exceptions and source-specific capabilities.

5

Finish interaction consistency

Unify save contracts, visual hierarchy, disclosures, action placement, themed sliders and touch feedback. Check every landscape checkpoint and the actual frame; surface polish follows the structural changes.

The acceptance test is a set of ordinary tasks

  1. “Make this clock bigger and move it.” Complete this visually, without numbers, with a large enough canvas and recoverable mistakes.
  2. “Keep photos from the last 90 days, but always keep these three.” Choose one understandable policy, protect selected pictures, and see what pruning will affect.
  3. “I never want this picture shown again.” Exclude from a selected-photo action; understand that refresh will not restore it; find a deliberate restore route.
  4. “Make the frame quiet at night.” Understand what happens to theme, brightness, photos, private content and Momo without reconciling disconnected forms.
  5. “Connect my photo account or calendar.” Enter private information on the frame in a focused flow, and see verified success or a specific actionable failure.
  6. “Pause private content now.” Find it quickly without scrolling through exports and library preferences.
  7. “Recover wireless updates after a restart.” Reach the Android shortcut and relevant instructions through a recognizable Frame destination.
  8. “Did that change save?” Understand persistence at the point of interaction; closing a draft must not silently masquerade as saving.

The target feeling: Skylit has already made the routine decisions well. Settings lets me express preferences, personalize the frame, and solve occasional problems without learning its internal model.
