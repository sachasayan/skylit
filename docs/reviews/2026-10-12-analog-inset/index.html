## Construction, material, light, and figure/ground

**Observed:** The outer tile is a softly rounded, thick-looking ivory panel. Its top edge catches light, and a soft shadow below/right separates it from the surrounding beige background. The circle inside is a different construction: a shallow **inset/recessed dial**, cut into the panel. It has no thick projecting bezel, separate mounting disk, or conspicuous outer drop shadow. A thin shaded rim at its upper/left interior transitions into the face; the lower/right boundary carries a delicate pale highlight. The upper interior shade and lower interior light create the concave reading, while the outer panel's edge/shadow creates the convex reading. Those two depth directions must remain distinguishable.

The face is warm ivory/beige, close to the panel's color, with soft tonal variation and faint material grain rather than a flat bright white fill. Its edge is a continuous quiet circle, neither a hard dark outline nor a strong decorative ring. The dial is large and lies behind the plant at lower right. The circle remains legible as one recess despite this occlusion.

**Interpretation and uncertainty:** The depth is conveyed through painted lighting rather than measurable geometry. A lightly embossed disk could be an alternative reading if considering only portions of the lower edge. Considering the darker upper interior edge, absence of a projecting bezel, and pale lower edge together supports the user's inset interpretation. The exact light source direction and material (plaster, ceramic, painted wood, etc.) cannot be established from the image. “Matte, warm, softly textured surface” is an appearance observation; a particular physical material is inference.

The hands and pivot appear to stand above the recessed face: soft offset shadows make them substantial without making the whole dial protrude. Dial marks/numerals appear painted or printed on the face. The visual hierarchy is panel → recessed face → hands/cap → foreground plant and shelf; these layers do not all share one identical shadow treatment.

## Dial marks and numerals

There are twelve sparse hour positions. Only 12, 3, 6, and 9 receive numerals; every hour position also has one simple short radial tick. There are no minute dots, dense minute subdivisions, filled hour wedges, or additional text inside the circle.

The ticks are thin warm brown strokes with gently rounded ends. Cardinal ticks point vertically/horizontally; diagonal ticks rotate radially. Their lengths are roughly 8–10% of the dial radius, with a much thinner stroke than either main hand. Tick outer ends sit about 90–92% of the radius from the center, and inner ends about 81–83%. Their radial spacing is regular even where foliage partly obscures the lower-right face.

Numerals are restrained warm brown, narrow-to-normal width, clean upright forms, roughly 12–13% of the radius tall. They are not heavy display typography. The visible 1 is slender, 2 curved/diagonal, 3 has two open bowls, 6 and 9 have rounded bowls. No obvious serif is visible at this scale. Numeral centers sit approximately 68–71% of the radius from the pivot, clearly inward of the ticks; 12 is centered as a two-character group. All four numbers read as one quiet typographic system. Exact font family, stroke modulation, and tiny terminal shape are uncertain at the supplied resolution.

## Hands and pivot proportions

The hour and minute hands are solid copper/terracotta brown, straight bars with rounded tips. They broaden the visual weight relative to the marks. They are not outline hands, needles, pointed spearheads, or long rectangles extending behind the pivot. Their visible bases disappear underneath a circular cap.

In this rendering the shorter hand reaches about 57–60% of the radius, and the longer about 69–72%. The shorter hand is visibly thicker: approximately 4–5% of the radius versus 3–4% for the longer. The longer hand still ends well inside the numeral/tick region rather than reaching the perimeter. The rounded ends and difference in width/length matter more than exact pixel measurements. Warm edge highlights and soft shadows give the hands shallow physical thickness.

A third, olive seconds hand is much finer, around 1–1.5% of the radius wide and about 66–69% of the radius long in this image. It is straight, unadorned, and has no large counterweight, lollipop, or conspicuous tail on the opposite side. It is visually subordinate to the copper pair.

The center is an olive circular cap, about 13–14% of the radius in diameter (roughly 6–7% of dial diameter). It covers the intersection completely. A subtle lighter upper edge and darker/shadowed lower edge make it a small domed or thick disk rather than a flat dot. There is no visible central screw, hole, concentric decorative rings, or copper hub. The exact dome profile is inference; its circular, olive, overlapping, slightly dimensional appearance is directly observable.

Reference hand directions merely show the styles at one instant. A correctly running clock may use entirely different angles. Compare length, thickness, caps, layering, and shadows after mentally rotating the hands to equivalent orientations.

## Plant: branching, leaves, and layer character

This is a leafy trailing houseplant, not a sparse upright sprig. Its roots/stems emerge from a clustered base at the pot opening on the right. Several short upright/arching stems support an upper cluster, while one or more thin, curved trailing stems descend leftward and then downward in front of the shelf. The tallest leaves reach into the dial's lower-right quadrant; the trailing leaves continue to the panel's bottom and are cropped there. These two growth directions are important to its silhouette.

Leaves are broad, asymmetric heart/ovate shapes with pointed tips, often a visible inward notch at the stem end. They are not identical thin almond leaves repeated at equal spacing. Leaves vary in size, orientation, visible face, and angle; some are twisted or partly hidden. Their attachment alternates irregularly along curved stems and bunches more densely near the pot. Several overlaps make the plant volumetric rather than a single evenly spaced flat branch.

The palette ranges from dark/mid olive to lighter yellow-green. Leaf faces carry subdued tonal differences and fine veins, especially a central vein; tips/edges remain soft and natural. A few leaves read as folded or bent through split tones. Stems are fine olive/brown curves, appreciably narrower than leaves. Botanical identification as pothos is plausible from the heart-shaped trailing foliage, but is not necessary or certain.

The plant occupies the foreground: some leaves cross the dial boundary, some overlap other leaves/stems, and the trailing foliage crosses the shelf/front and panel's lower edge. It does not look like a transparent plant watermark, a uniform vector icon, or a freestanding ornamental branch centered in a pot.

## Pot, contact, and shelf structure

The pot is warm orange terracotta with visible softly mottled/clay-like texture, lit lighter toward its front-left and darker at its right/edges. It is cropped by the panel's right boundary. The opening is not just a flat line: stems enter a small dark/occluded top region, and a distinct thick upper lip/rim encircles it. The lip projects beyond the body and casts a narrow darker band below. The pot body is rounded/tapered, wider toward the upper body and narrowing toward the base. A lower curved foot/base band is separately legible rather than an abrupt rectangular bottom.

The pot sits on the wooden shelf; its base meets the shelf top with a small contact shadow. It should not float, pass through the plank, or terminate on a completely unrelated baseline. Exact back-side rim geometry is obscured by foliage and cropping, so do not require a fully visible ellipse that the reference does not show.

The shelf is a short light natural-wood plank entering from the lower-right edge. It has a horizontal top surface, a visible front fascia of nonzero thickness, softly rounded/beveled edges, and restrained horizontal wood grain. At the left end the shelf starts around the lower-right half of the dial, below the dial's bottom region. It continues behind the pot and is clipped at the panel edge. A subtle underside shadow separates it from the panel, and a small under-plank/support-like vertical section is visible near its left side. That section's exact furniture construction is ambiguous; it should not be asserted as a specific bracket type. Trailing stems/leaves partly conceal the plank/support.

## Composition and identity-bearing omissions

The large dial dominates the upper/left tile. The plant/pot/shelf form a smaller, dense asymmetrical vignette at lower right that visibly overlaps the clock rather than sitting in an isolated adjacent column. The pivot is above and left of the vignette's center. Open face around the hands/numerals provides breathing room; detail is concentrated at the lower-right edge. The panel's rounded corner crops the vignette naturally. Dial and panel share a close warm light palette, while copper hands/marks and olive foliage/cap provide restrained contrast.

Omissions that change the reference's identity:

- Replacing the shallow recess with a raised disk, thick bezel, or generic flat circle.
- Losing the distinct depth relationship between outer panel, inset face, raised hands/cap, and foreground vignette.
- Replacing rounded copper bars and a small dimensional olive cap with generic pointed hands or a flat hub.
- Adding a dense minute scale or changing the four quiet numerals into large/heavy decorative typography.
- Replacing the clustered heart-leaf trailing plant with a sparse, uniformly repeated upright branch.
- Losing the visibly projecting pot lip, rounded taper/base, contact shadow, or plank thickness/wood structure.
- Separating plant and clock so their overlap no longer creates the integrated lower-right composition.

## Discriminating visual tests for later comparison

1. **Concavity test:** Hide hands/foliage mentally and ask whether the dial is an indentation in the panel. Inspect upper inner shading versus lower inner highlight, absence of a heavy external shadow, and absence of a raised perimeter. A ring existing at all is insufficient.
2. **Layer test:** Trace the panel → recess → marks → hand bars → olive cap, and face → stems/leaves → shelf/pot contacts. Check whether overlaps and shadow directions agree with those layers.
3. **Proportion test:** Normalize to dial radius and compare tick band, numeral band, relative hand lengths/widths, and cap diameter. Do not penalize a different clock angle or require fixed tile coordinates.
4. **Silhouette test:** Blur/squint at the vignette. It should retain an upper clustered mass and irregular broad-leaf cascade descending left/down, with the pot at right and shelf below. A generic upright plant passes an object checklist but fails this silhouette test.
5. **Leaf variety test:** Inspect several leaves for heart/ovate shape, pointed tips, differing sizes/orientations, vein/fold shading, and irregular overlap. Repetition should not dominate the plant.
6. **Pot/shelf contact test:** Follow stems into the rim, rim into tapered body/foot, foot onto shelf top, and shelf top into front/underside. The sequence should feel physically coherent and include occlusion/contact shading.
7. **Figure/ground test:** At both whole-tile and close inspection scales, the dial should remain a quiet large recess while copper hands and olive foliage carry detail. Heavy borders or stronger decorative gradients must not overtake that hierarchy.

A raised ring is not required to communicate the dial: the faint inner shade/highlight is the defining evidence. The trailing plant is also substantially denser and broader-leafed than a simple botanical sprig. Proportions above are approximate, internally checked against the visible circle; they are not claims of exact source geometry. Material names, botanical species, cap dome shape, and the shelf's small underside structure remain uncertain. The inventory is now locked for comparison before seeing implementation.
